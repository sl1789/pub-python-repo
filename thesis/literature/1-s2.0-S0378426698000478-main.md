# Nandi (1998) — *How Important Is the Correlation Between Returns and Volatility in a Stochastic Volatility Model?*

- **PDF:** `1-s2.0-S0378426698000478-main.pdf`
- **Venue:** *Journal of Banking & Finance*, Vol. 22, pp. 589–610.

## Methodology (Monte Carlo variant used)
Empirical test of the **closed-form Heston (1993) stochastic-volatility
model** on S&P 500 index options, comparing two restrictions:

- the **unconstrained** version with non-zero spot–vol correlation $\rho$,
- the **constrained** version with $\rho = 0$.

The Heston model prices European options via **inverse Fourier transform
of the characteristic function** (Heston 1993). Monte Carlo is used only
as a benchmark for the analytical formula and for hedging error
simulation — it is not the primary pricing engine. Estimation of the
parameter vector $(\kappa, \theta, \xi, \rho, v_0)$ is by nonlinear
least squares on cross-sectional option prices, repeated daily.

Hedging performance is evaluated by simulating discrete-time
delta-hedge portfolios and reporting absolute hedging errors out of
sample.

## Type of data
**Empirical.** S&P 500 index option prices (intraday quotes), with
matched index level and dividend yield. Sample restricted to liquid OTM
and ATM contracts.

## Computational setup
**Single machine.** Closed-form pricing plus daily nonlinear LS; no
parallel infrastructure required.

## Key contribution
Quantifies the **practical cost** of imposing $\rho = 0$:

- Pricing: non-zero $\rho$ significantly reduces OTM mispricing and
  improves overall fit.
- Hedging: non-zero $\rho$ produces significantly lower hedging errors
  for OTM options.

In other words, the negative leverage correlation observed empirically
is **economically meaningful**, not a cosmetic refinement.

## Strengths
- Direct test of an analytically tractable SV model on a deep, liquid
  option market.
- Distinguishes pricing performance from hedging performance — both
  matter to practitioners and they can disagree.
- Frame is fully consistent with the BS limit ($\xi = 0$).

## Limitations
- Heston is a one-factor SV model and cannot match the full term
  structure of implied volatilities simultaneously with the smile across
  strikes; multi-factor or jump-diffusion extensions improve the fit.
- No jumps, so left-tail crash risk is bundled into the diffusion.
- Daily re-calibration is required to maintain fit — a clear sign of
  model misspecification.
- No formal goodness-of-fit test beyond mean pricing/hedging errors.

# Coleman, Ghattamaneni, Logan, Labouseur — *Computational Finance with Map-Reduce in Scala*

- **PDF:** `PDP2627.pdf`
- **Venue:** PDP conference proceedings (Marist College, Computer Science Department).

## Methodology (no Monte Carlo — deterministic pricing)
The "computational finance" workload is **bond portfolio valuation**,
not option pricing under uncertainty. The fair value of a simple bond
is computed in closed form via the discounted-cash-flow formula

$$
P(b_i, r) = \sum_{t=1}^{T \times n} \frac{C}{(1+r)^{t/n}} + \frac{M}{(1+r)^T},
$$

with the U.S. Treasury on-the-run yield curve interpolated by polynomial
curve fitting. Portfolio value is the sum of constituent bond prices.
There is **no Monte Carlo**: the computation is deterministic; the
parallelism question is purely about throughput.

Four parallel algorithms are evaluated against two serial baselines:

1. **Naïve** — one portfolio per map task.
2. **Memory-bound** — pre-load all bonds, then map over portfolios.
3. **Coarse-grain** — explicit caps on the number of mappers / reducers
   tied to the available hardware threads.
4. **Fine-grain** — one bond per map task instead of one portfolio.

## Type of data
**Synthetic.** 5,000 simple bonds and 100,000 portfolios are generated
from random parameter grids (coupon, maturity, frequency) and stored
in MongoDB.

## Computational setup
**Single-node multicore parallelism via the Scala Actor model**, exposed
through a pure-Scala map-reduce API (Haller–Sommers). Tested on three
Intel multicore platforms (Xeon W3540, Core i5-650, Core 2 Duo T3300).
**No Hadoop, no Spark, no cluster** — the parallelism target is multi-
core / hyper-threaded hardware on a single workstation.

Performance metrics reported: speed-up $R = T_1 / T_N$ and efficiency
$e = R / N$, with $N$ the number of execution units.

## Key contribution
- **Empirical demonstration of super-linear speed-up and
  super-efficiency** (efficiency > 100%) on bond-portfolio valuation,
  attributed to effective exploitation of hardware hyper-threads by the
  Scala actor model.
- Observation that the "naïve" coarse-grained mapping (one portfolio per
  map task) is essentially as good as more sophisticated fine-grain or
  pre-loaded variants — overhead and locality dominate any algorithmic
  refinement.
- Compute–I/O overlap evidence that explains where the super-linear
  speed-up comes from in practice.

## Strengths
- Concrete, reproducible methodology with code available online.
- Direct, side-by-side comparison of four parallelisation strategies
  on three distinct CPU architectures.
- Uses a fully end-to-end workflow including database I/O, not just
  isolated compute.

## Limitations
- The chosen workload (bond DCF pricing) is **embarrassingly parallel
  and analytically closed-form**; it does not stress-test fault
  tolerance, shuffle, or cross-partition aggregation as a Monte Carlo
  pricing workload would.
- No Monte Carlo simulation, no risk metrics, no path-dependent
  derivatives — limits transferability of the conclusions to options
  pricing.
- Single-node multicore only; no comparison to Spark, Dask, or
  cluster-level map-reduce.
- Reported super-efficiency is plausibly an artefact of cache-warming
  or background OS load on the test machines; statistical confidence
  intervals are not reported.
